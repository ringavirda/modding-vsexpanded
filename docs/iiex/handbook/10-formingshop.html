The forming shop is where iron stops being a lump and starts being parts. A furnace hands you a bloom, a shingled bar or a cast slab, and every machine in this article works that raw shape down until a recipe can name it.

None of them are swung by hand. Each one takes its power from a line of Cast Iron Shaft turned by a Flywheel, and each takes drive on the two faces along its own shaft, so a row of machines chains onto a single line and runs off a single drive. A machine on a stopped run refuses to work rather than working slowly, and a run that cannot carry what is hung on it stalls outright, so lay in the drive before you fill the line with machines.

The Rolling Mill is the reducing machine, and the only one. Fit it with a roll set - rightmouse with the set in hand - and it thins whatever you feed it one gauge at a time: the Flat Roll Set rolls plate, the Grooved Roll Set rolls round bar, and the Wide Flat Roll Set takes the widest plate the shop makes. Lay stock on the deck and rightmouse the near or the far side of it to choose which gap the piece goes through. The mill never makes a finished piece, only thinner stock, which is what keeps it a single-purpose machine and sends everything on to the shear. Stock that has gone cold is refused outright: take it back to the heat.

The Crop Shear is where a rolling schedule ends. Seat a blade set in the nest and rightmouse stock against it: one stroke takes one finished piece off and hands the rest back as stock at the same gauge, so a long bar becomes several parts and a shorter bar. A piece you have started cutting cannot go back through the mill - finish the cuts, then roll the pieces on. A cold cut is allowed and asks more of the drive than a hot one, so a heavy enough flywheel buys you the freedom to cut without reheating first.

The Nail Cutter and the Riveter are the two fastener benches, and they are the same machine wearing different tooling. Fit a Nail Die or a Rivet Die and rightmouse the bench with the blank it wants. A die names the machine it belongs to, so a nail die fitted to a riveter simply has no work and says so. The nail cutter cuts a rolled nail plate into four bundles of nails and strips; the riveter upsets a rivet rod into two bundles of rivets. Rivets go twice as far - eight bundles of rivets out of the iron that yields four bundles of nails - which is why the nail cutter is deliberately the cheaper machine to build. Wherever a joint only has to hold, either fastener will do; wherever it also has to be tight, a boiler shell above all, only rivets are accepted.

Stock cools while you work it, and a forge tops out at an ingot. The Heating Furnace is the shop's answer: build it up from its core, lay pieces along its hearth rows, and light the firebox. Anything lying on the hearth climbs back to rolling heat while you work at the machines, and a thin piece comes back faster than a thick one, since it is the surface that takes the heat. Long stock keeps in a Storage Rack, which fills by the length of what you lean in it rather than by the piece.

A working shop is therefore four things standing together: a drive strong enough for everything hung on it, a mill to reduce, a shear to crop, and a heat beside them so nothing has to be carried away to stay workable. Add a bench once you want fasteners rather than parts, and the line feeds the boilers and engines that drive it.
