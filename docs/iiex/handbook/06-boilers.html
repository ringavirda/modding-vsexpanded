A boiler burns coal under a water vessel and forces the resulting steam into a pipe network; every litre of water boils into 16 L of steam, hot enough to read about 157 °C at the pressure a Cornish boiler will hold. To build one, place the boiler block and right-click it (rightmouse) repeatedly with the required materials in your hotbar to raise it through its construction stages. It brings its own brickwork, grate and hatches with it - there is no masonry to wall in around it and nothing to fit outside the space it reserves.

The Cornish Boiler is the one to start with - a simple single-flue boiler that supplies low-pressure steam, enough to get your first machines turning. Raising it consumes 22 metal plates, 8 rods and 32 rivets (iron or steel) together with 44 fire bricks. Rivets, not nails: a riveted seam is the one joint that is both strong and tight, and no stage of a pressure vessel will take anything else. It pushes up to 64 L/s of steam and chokes at 5 atm; its vessel holds 1600 L but needs at least 300 L present before it will boil.

The boiler answers on two hatches. The main hatch is the firing door at the near end: hold rightmouse with an empty hand to swing it, right-click coal into the open door to charge the grate, and hold rightmouse once more on a full grate to set it alight. The man hatch is the cover on top of the barrel: hold to swing it, then pour water in from a bucket or bail it back out. Steam escapes only through the man hatch, which makes it both your emergency blow-off and the one thing to keep shut while the boiler is working.

The grate holds four courses of four - sixteen units of a single coal, which you can read off the block as it burns down. Anything that burns hot enough will fire it: coke, charcoal, anthracite, black coal, and even lignite. What separates them is not flame but how long a charge lasts: a grate of anthracite runs the better part of an hour, black coal about twenty minutes, and coke or charcoal about ten. Only lignite burns too cool to reach the boiler's full rate, and it costs about a tenth of it.

Getting a boiler running is a manual affair at first. You prime it by hand with buckets through the man hatch, and once it is making steam that steam can drive the engine behind a fluid pump, which then refills the vessel automatically - the trick is to reach the pressure your engine needs before the hand-poured water runs dry. Once that loop catches, the pumping engine keeps your boilers topped up for as long as the coal lasts. Feedwater couples to the south face of the boiler block itself; steam leaves through the port on top of the barrel, and the flue gas through the port at the far corner. All the while the fire must breathe: vent that exhaust port through a chimney or pipe, because if the back-pressure there climbs above ~0.8 atm the fire goes out. After about three minutes of heating the boiler starts to boil and sends steam toward your engines.

Boilers are dangerous. One that keeps firing with nowhere to send its steam sits at its choke pressure and can explode. Your emergency release is the man hatch - opening it vents steam harmlessly at around 200 L/s. An unpiped steam outlet merely leaks, which is not enough to save an over-pressured boiler, and feeding pressurised water into a boiling vessel flashes it to extra steam and spikes the pressure, so always throttle or gate any pumped feedwater. Beware in particular that a Cornish boiler's 5 atm ceiling sits above the Watt engine's 4 atm breaking point: put a pressure valve between them, with an overflow pipe, to keep the engine safe.
