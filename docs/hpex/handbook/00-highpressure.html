High Pressure Expanded adds the top of the steam chain: a bigger boiler that raises steam into the high band, and an engine efficient enough to spend it well. Both are steel builds and both assume the low-pressure loop already works - read Steam Power: Boilers and Steam Power: Engines first, because everything there about construction stages, fireboxes, priming, sub-machines and repairs applies here unchanged.

The larger Lancashire Boiler is a twin-flue design that delivers higher pressures and greater volumes for the more demanding machines, though be ready for the steel it costs. It takes 36 steel plates, 24 nails-and-strips and 12 rods with 64 fire bricks, and its firebox uses 39 fire-brick blocks plus the same door, passthroughs and outlet as the Cornish. It reaches up to 48 L/s and 12 atm, with a 1200 L vessel that needs 200 L to begin boiling.

The Cornish Engine is the more advanced design, working at higher pressure and wringing the same power from less steam because it can regulate how much steam enters the cylinder. Running at 6-8 atm, its control rod sets a working rate of Low, Normal or High that draws 8, 16 or 32 L/s of steam and delivers 0.2, 0.4 or 0.8 units of power respectively (an internal value); on High it will comfortably drive six helve hammers, and that same setting also scales how much water an attached pump moves. Its construction stages cost the same materials as the Watt engine - the difference in price lies in the engine's main block.

The two are built for each other, but not blindly: the Lancashire's 12 atm ceiling sits well above the Cornish engine's 8 atm breaking point, so put a pressure valve between them, with an overflow pipe, exactly as you would between a Cornish boiler and a Watt engine. Throttling the engine up raises the pressure it needs to engage as well as the steam it drinks, so a line that runs a Low setting comfortably may sit below the High setting's engage pressure entirely.
