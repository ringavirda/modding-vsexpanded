The Blast Furnace is the heart of the whole operation: a tall multiblock built mostly from refractory brick, with two tuyeres (blast intakes) low on the shaft, two gas outlets high up and a sealed top crowned by its two hoppers. Place the Blast Furnace Core first - it is the control block, sitting at the bottom centre of the hearth floor - then hold ctrl + shift + rightmouse on it to see the build outline and fill in every highlighted block until the structure reports itself complete. All told it needs 99 tier-3 refractory bricks - exactly tier 3, no lesser brick will do - plus the core, two tuyeres, an Iron Tap low in one wall with a Slag Tap one course higher in the opposite wall, two Pipe Outlets for the exhaust, a Reinforced Hopper and a Bell Hopper. The hollow shaft inside is left empty - that is where the charge goes. Mind the facings on the taps and tuyeres: a part fitted the wrong way round will not complete the structure, so turn it until the outline accepts it.

The furnace does not eat ore. It eats burden - crushed iron ore fluxed with lime, made at an Ironworking Expanded Burdenmaker - and it eats coke, and the two go in as separate, alternating layers; nothing is premixed. The Reinforced Hopper on top is the loading tank, and it holds one material at a time on purpose: one load lays one layer. rightmouse with charge in hand adds one unit, ctrl + rightmouse adds the whole stack, and an empty hand draws the tank back out. The Bell Hopper below pulls from the tank and drips the charge down the shaft, always into the lowest column, so the layers lie level on their own; dropping is on by default, an empty-handed ctrl + rightmouse on the hopper toggles it, and it stops by itself once the shaft is full. So you charge in rounds: fill the tank with coke and let it drain, then with burden, then coke again. Coke is baked from coal in a coke oven - raw coal is no fuel here at all - and charcoal is accepted at half the carbon, so a shaft charged with it runs cooler. Crush your ore in a pulverizer; even scrap iron bits crush back into crushed iron and go round again.

There is no door and no torch. The furnace lights itself the moment a complete course of fuel stands at the bottom of the shaft, in front of the tuyeres - which is why coke goes down first. Burden on its own cannot burn, and coke can never be slipped in beneath burden that is already laid, so a charge started with burden will never catch; if that happens, take the charge back out (an empty-handed rightmouse on a charge pile in the shaft takes a band off the top, and breaking a pile drops its contents) and start over with fuel. Once lit, the fire lives in front of the tuyeres, and the coke burns whether or not iron is being made - a lit furnace left standing is spending its charge on nothing.

To melt, the fire needs air and heat. The tuyeres drink pressurised air off the pipe network, and how much pressure the charge demands depends on how it was laid - a coke-lean charge packs dense and must be blown much harder. On plain cold air a standard charge stalls short of iron's melting line of about 1482 °C; hot blast is what carries it past. Charge a Cowper Stove on the furnace's own exhaust and route the blast through it - the Hot Blast article covers that half of the plant - and the furnace enters the melting phase, rendering the burden into molten pig iron and slag. Point at the core while it runs: block-info shows what the furnace is doing, the temperature against the threshold it must clear, both sides of the heat ledger and the grade of the burden, so a stall reads as a number and not a mystery. One stall deserves its own name: an under-fuelled charge arrives at the tuyeres too cold to melt and hangs - the furnace stays lit and hot, keeps eating its coke, and simply stops making iron. The core reports the hang; break the cold piles out of the bottom and charge richer.

Molten pig iron pools behind the lower tap and slag behind the upper one, and each tap needs the start of a molten canal beneath its spout before an empty-handed rightmouse will toggle the pour. Keep the taps open while it melts: the pools have a ceiling, and anything rendered over a full pool is lost. The fire itself ends a few ways. It goes out when the fuel at the tuyeres runs out - a campaign lasts exactly as long as the coke you laid - and it smothers if the exhaust backs up, so keep the outlets flowing through the cowpers and the smoke stack. Losing the blast does not kill it; the furnace falls back to a cool natural-draught burn, too cold to melt, until the air returns. Breaking a wall lets it burn on through the hole, but a breached furnace can never relight until it is repaired. And when it does go out, whatever was still molten freezes onto the hearth floor as solidified pig to be mined back out, while the unburnt charge stays standing in the shaft - dig it out through the piles, and expect what is left to be richer in coke toward the top.
