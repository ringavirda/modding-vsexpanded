Hot blast is preheated, pressurised air, and it is what lets the furnace climb hot enough to actually melt iron. A bare blast furnace tops out around 1400 °C, short of iron's melting point; to push past that you reclaim the heat the furnace throws away in its exhaust and feed it back in through the air. For this to work the air you supply must be both pressurised above about 2.5 atm and heated above roughly 800 °C.

Everything breathes through pipes from Low Pressure Expanded, and here a network carries one of two gases: ordinary air, and the hot exhaust that spills from the furnace. A handful of machines move it around. The Air Blower is a steam-engine sub-machine that pressurises air and forces it through the cowper stoves and into the furnace tuyeres. Valves open and close a line by hand, which you will need constantly for swapping the cowper stoves over and steering flow through the network, while pressure valves open by themselves above their gate pressure to spill overflow to another side (their input side wears a copper-textured rim). Outlets connect a network to a structure's face and form part of its multiblock footprint. Each network tracks its gas type, volume and temperature, and any open-ended pipe leaks, so plan how many machines you run carefully to avoid under- or over-supplying.

The Cowper Stove is a heat regenerator, and it is what makes the air hot. Pipe the furnace's exhaust into a stove and it soaks that heat into the heatsink at its core - \"charging\" - which you can speed up by burning a coal pile inside, fastest of all with anthracite; just beware that burning low grades of coal makes the stove itself give off exhaust from its main output. Once charged, route plain pressurised air through the same stove and it pours out scorching hot, and fed into the tuyeres it drives the furnace's internal temperature back up toward about 1700 °C. Each stove needs 48 refractory bricks of any tier, a cowper stove intake as its control block, four heat sinks, two pipe outlets, one straight pipe passthrough and an iron hatch door, with one slot below the intake left open for the coal pile that speeds the exchange.

A single stove can only do one job at a time, and a charged core runs down as you draw heat from it, so you run two stoves as a pair: while one charges on exhaust, the other discharges hot air to the furnace, and when the discharging one drops below 800 °C you swap their roles with valves. Keep exhaust and air on separate paths, because if they mix inside a stove it cannot heat up.

A blast furnace also makes more exhaust than the stoves can swallow, and a backed-up exhaust line will choke and extinguish it, so the Smoke Stack vents the surplus to the sky and acts as the safety valve of the whole system. It needs a smoke stack intake as its control block, 24 refractory bricks of any tier and 36 of any ordinary brick. Above all, keep an eye on the stoves' temperature - let them cool and the furnace will stop smelting or go out entirely.
