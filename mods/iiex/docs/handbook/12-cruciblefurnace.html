Crucible steel is the best steel the ironworks makes: melted whole in a sealed pot, so it comes out with no slag streaks and no soft spots. It is what a cutting edge wants. The Crucible Furnace is a deep hole in the floor with coke packed round four lidded pots, and everything about running it comes down to one lever.

Start with the feedstock. Blister steel is what goes in the pot, and it goes in cold. Set a cold blister ingot on an anvil and the helve hammer crushes it into chunks and bits instead of forging it - a whole ingot comes back as three chunks and five bits. Heat that same ingot first and nothing changes about the old route: it forges into shear steel exactly as it always did. One ingot, two products, and the only thing that picks between them is whether the metal is hot.

A pot takes 110 units - four chunks and two bits, which is a little more than one ingot's crush, and the difference is the melt loss you get back as 100 units of finished steel. The pot itself is fire clay, clayformed and fired in a pit kiln like any other clay vessel; ordinary clay will not survive what happens next, which is why the recipe asks for fire clay and nothing else. A pot gives three heats and then breaks after its last pour. That is not a fault, it is the cost of the process - keep making pots.

Now the chimney, which is the whole temperature control. This furnace has no blower and never will: the only thing pulling air through the coke is the stack above it, so how tall you build it is how hot the furnace gets. The layout gives you the flue and one course of the chimney; the rest is yours to lay, a ring of brick round an open middle, course on course. Read the core and it tells you how many courses it counts, what they are pulling, and how many it would take to reach the best a chimney can do. Past that peak a taller stack pulls less - the flue loses more heat than the extra height gains - and the block says so rather than leaving you guessing.

With the furnace built, seat your pots and charge them, fill the hearth with coke, and light it with the damper shut. A cold pot taken straight into full draught cracks, so the shut damper is the gentle fire that brings it up. Watch the block: each hole reports how far through its warming it is.

When every pot has come up, open the damper. That is the one deliberate act in a heat. Full draught, the fire climbs past 1600 °C, and the pots melt - each on its own clock, so a pot you seated late finishes late. Open it too early and you lose those pots; the charge falls through into the ash pit below and you can pick it back up, but the pots are gone.

Pull a finished pot with an empty hand and you get a crucible you can pour. Take it to a cast-iron ingot mould - itself sand-cast, like the rest of the casting line - and pour: one pot fills one mould exactly, and what you lift out is an ingot of crucible steel.

It makes tools and weapons that last longer than anything else the works turns out. It gates nothing: a wrought-iron head still does every job it ever did. This is simply the better one.
